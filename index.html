Overview

Network centralization and collective adaptability with LLM agents

The original study

Bernstein, Shore, and Jang (2023, Organization Science) studied how the structure of a communication network affects a group's ability to adapt when its environment changes. Groups of nine participants recruited on Amazon Mechanical Turk solved a fictional murder mystery in 15 minutes. Each participant searched for clues, could message the network neighbours they were connected to, and entered answers to "Who is the victim?" and "Who is the murderer?". Pay depended on how long a correct answer was entered.

The environment shifts during the game: early clues point to a plausible but wrong suspect (the red herring), and clues released after five minutes, together with broadcasts at five and ten minutes, identify the actual murderer. Groups were assigned to one of six network treatments (hub and spoke, core-periphery, in core-periphery, out core-periphery, locally clustered, complete clique), about 30 groups each, and compared with synthetic groups of isolates, formed from participants who could not observe any other participant. The study tested four hypotheses on whether centralized networks adapt better than decentralized ones (H1 to H4).

This replication

Every participant is replaced by a language-model agent (gpt-6-luna). The task, clues, timing, network treatments, and payment rules follow the original study. Agents act in 30 turns of 30 seconds; in each turn they search, send messages, enter answers, and write private notes. Agent settings were calibrated against the human data and then fixed (see "Agent settings" on the Replication page).

The final settings were run with 10 trials per original network treatment, 10 additional trials for the out and in core-periphery networks, and 10 trials with isolates. Sixteen counterfactual variants of the two one-way core-periphery networks (number of ties between core and periphery, core size, periphery size, star shape) were run with 20 trials each.
