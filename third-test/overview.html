Synthetic organisations · third test

Brandts & Cooper Replication

A manager and two agents must coordinate on one of five actions. The agents know which action is best for the group, but each prefers a different one, and the manager does not know. The paper varies who decides and how the three can talk, across seven treatments. This page explains the setting and every treatment; the full specification is in APPROACH.md.

Why this paper

In the first test, agents with a private incentive argued for their side but never misstated what they knew. Here misreporting is built into the organisation: the people who hold the information gain from distorting it, and the person who decides cannot check it until the round is over. The paper varies the two basic levers of organisation design, the allocation of the decision right and the channel of communication, and keeps everything else fixed. That makes it a direct test of whether synthetic organisations respond to design the way human ones do.

Headline result to replicate

Only the combination of managerial control and free-form chat significantly beats the safe outcome, because in chat agents almost stop lying to their manager (false game reports in 1% of agent-rounds, against 42% with structured messages, in rounds 10 to 18). Non-reporting rounds remain in the denominator.

The setting

Three players stay together for 18 rounds. The instructions call them F, C and A; the paper calls them agent 1, agent 2 and the manager. Each round the computer draws one of five games. Each game is a payoff table with the same five rows and five columns; only the numbers differ.

Payoffs

Every table follows one rule, so the five tables differ only in where the group's best cell sits. Each agent loses for not matching the other agent (most), for being far from its own favourite, and for being far from the drawn game (least):

Two benchmarks for every group.

Efficient: both play the game's own number. Total surplus is 80 every round, but the agents must change their actions as the game changes. Under managerial control, A must also acquire their information. Safe: both always play 3. It needs no information, gives F and C equal payoffs, and is each agent's maximin choice. It yields 64 in games 1 and 5, 72 in games 2 and 4, and 80 in game 3. Under managerial control, theory predicts the safe outcome: each agent gains from steering the manager toward its favourite, so pure-strategy equilibria are babbling equilibria and the manager learns nothing. Results are scored as the efficiency gain: 0% is the safe outcome, 100% is efficient, below 0% means surplus below the safe benchmark. A coordinated but poorly chosen outcome can also fall below that benchmark.

One round

All treatments share this sequence. Only step 3 differs between treatments, and step 4 differs in who chooses. Earnings add up over all 18 rounds (1 ECU = €0.02, plus €5 for showing up).

Why the follow-up matters.

Chat differs from structured reports in several ways: agents can call out a liar, the manager can ask for the truth, agents can vouch for themselves, reports are asynchronous, and reporting is optional. EC-MC adds all five to structured messages. It removed less than half of the gap in lying, suggesting that free-form communication itself matters. With agents we can test the same mechanisms. The human study used different time budgets; equal turn budgets in the synthetic study are an operational adaptation.

Two other findings matter for the comparison. Human managers in SC-MC made avoidable errors: they did not follow two matching reports in 18% of cases, which wiped out the value of the information they did get. And in CH-MC, agents who did not tell the truth mostly stayed silent rather than lie.

What we test

  1. R1. In CH-MC, total surplus is above the safe outcome.
  2. R2. Total surplus is higher in CH-MC than in SC-MC.
  3. R3. The share of agent-rounds with a false game report is far lower in CH-MC than in SC-MC, including non-reporting rounds.
  4. R4. In SC-MC, lying falls as the truth becomes better for the agent; in CH-MC it does not.

Secondary: NC-D lowest; the ordering of all seven treatments; EC-MC between SC-MC and CH-MC in lying.

From humans to agents

Each player is one gpt-6-luna context that keeps its own history for all 18 rounds. Agents see the authors' instructions, the five payoff tables as text, and the neutral labels F, C and A; the words "manager" and "agent" never appear. Each group faces the exact game draws of the human groups: the authors used nine fixed 18-round sequences, one per group position in a session, in all 40 sessions of the package.

Risks and status

  • Honest agentsIf agents seldom lie in SC-MC, there is no lying gap for chat to close (R3, R4). The first test's agents never stated a self-serving falsehood. That would itself be a finding; we will not push agents to lie.
  • Competent managersAn agent manager that always follows matching reports could lift SC-MC above the humans for reasons unrelated to truth-telling. Manager errors are reported separately.
  • Focal safe outcomeWithout communication, agents may settle on 3 from round 1, which would make NC-D look better than in humans.
  • Prior knowledgeThe game circulated in working papers before 2025. Neutral labels make recognition less likely; we search transcripts for the authors' names and the game's name.
  • Response opportunitiesThe budget was raised from 5 to 8 turns after the xhigh pilot cut off EC-MC exchanges. Check that players can request information, challenge a claim and respond before choices.
