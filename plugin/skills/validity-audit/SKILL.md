# Examine the assumptions behind the argument

Use during design, construction, counterfactual exploration, and interpretation. Begin with the question, intended target, and proposed explanation. Help the researcher decide which assumptions about members, interactions, or the setting matter to the inference.

## Starting points for reflection

| Consideration | Questions it may raise |
|---|---|
| **Social preferences** | What interests or social motives does the explanation assume? How are they represented, and how would their influence be observed? |
| **Behavioural variation** | Which differences among members or dependencies among their judgements matter to the collective outcome? |
| **Memory and communication limits** | Does the explanation depend on what members can retain, attend to, process, or exchange? |
| **Private-information disclosure** | What does it assume about who knows, shares, withholds, or distorts information? |

These themes are examples. Consider other assumptions when the research question requires them; an assumption does not need a catalogue entry to matter. Differences are setting- and model-dependent, and need not be defects when AI organisations themselves are the subject.

## Guide the researcher's judgement

For a consequential assumption, explain why it matters, how the construction represents it, and what evidence could examine it. Start with the observations and source evidence already available. Suggest an additional diagnostic only when it addresses an unresolved uncertainty relevant to the claim.

Choose the diagnostic form from the question: it might involve observation of interactions, a comparison, a controlled change, or a task-specific check. Describe what different findings would imply and what the check cannot establish. Do not turn an example from a previous study into a universal requirement.

If a construction change is justified, make its rationale and expected consequences explicit. Follow [study protocol](../study-protocol/SKILL.md) for separating adjustment from evaluation and recording changes. A restored outcome alone does not establish the proposed explanation.

For a counterfactual arrangement, revisit which assumptions remain plausible and which are changed or newly introduced. A check passed in the reference setting does not automatically establish its applicability to the alternative.

Keep the evidence states distinct:

- **Potential threat:** An assumption may fail in this setting.
- **Observed deviation:** Behaviour or outcomes differ from a stated expectation or benchmark; the cause remains open.
- **Supported boundary:** Evidence supports a particular limit under the examined conditions, with alternatives and uncertainty stated.

High accuracy alone does not identify prosociality; an individual response does not establish its transfer to interacting organisations; and a passed diagnostic does not establish correspondence beyond what it examined.

## Supporting evidence

Open relevant files in references/modules/ only when they help a concrete research decision. The modules describe possible evidence and construction choices, not compulsory tests or completion gates.

The existing IDs help locate material: A1-A5 concern social dispositions and information exchange, B1-B7 concern cognition and elicitation, C1-C2 concern heterogeneity and dependence, and D1-D4 concern simulation-specific threats. Recognition, specification effects, and model drift may warrant attention when they could change the interpretation.

Use the study plan and report to record the assumptions selected, the evidence obtained, remaining uncertainties, and the scope of the resulting claims.
