Synthetic organisations · first test

Maciejovsky Replication

We test whether LLM agents reproduce an established organisational finding, and where they depart from it. The agents rerun Study 1 of Maciejovsky and Budescu (2020), in which three people hold different parts of the evidence on three job applicants and pool it either by discussing or by trading shares in a market: first its control conditions, then its conflict-of-interest manipulations. This page describes the design; the results are on a separate page.

Why we replicate first

Our paper uses synthetic organisations to build theory from counterfactuals, comparing matched versions of one situation that differ in their coordination arrangement. We can only rely on that comparison if synthetic agents reproduce a result already established with humans. Study 1 suits this check because both conditions give the same three roles the same evidence; they differ in how members exchange information and in how they are paid.

Replication hypothesis

Without conflicts of interest, discussion produces more correct final choices than the market.

Primary outcome: number of agents in a triad who rank the best applicant first after interacting (0 to 3). We also report unanimous and majority correctness separately. Conflict conditions and trust ratings are outside this first experiment.

Task and information

Each participant sees three of the five attribute ratings for every applicant, nine cues in total. All attributes carry equal weight, and every participant knows the scoring rule. B is the unique best applicant only on the pooled evidence.

This is a weak hidden profile.

Each participant already has B tied for first on their own cues (A = B for participants 1 and 2, B = C for participant 3). Pooling only the cues that two or more participants hold already picks B (A 3, B 5, C 2). With all evidence, A and C tie at 1, although the paper calls A second. Only "B ranked first" is well defined. Agents that apply the rule carefully may all rank B first in discussion, however little they share.

Procedure

Each triad starts with three new agents. Steps 1 to 4 and step 6 are the same in both conditions; step 5 differs. Agents are never told which applicant is best.

Architecture

The engines enforce the institutional rules in code; the prompts describe them to the agents. Each agent runs in its own context and receives only its own cues and what its institution shows it. The analysis is the only component that reads the answer key, and it uses the key for scoring.

Human benchmarks

The participant-level data (Data A) are not in the published supplements, so the vote means come from the paper's Figure 2 and the unanimity counts from Table 3. Each condition had 15 triads.

Analysis plan

The unit is the triad. A one-sided permutation test compares mean B votes (discussion > market), with a bootstrap 95% CI on the difference. Unanimous and majority correctness are reported as proportions with Wilson CIs and Fisher exact tests.

Risks and status

  • Ceiling in discussionConfirmed: all 50 discussion triads ended unanimous on B. Every triad had pooled the full profile by the end of round 2, so the ceiling reflects complete information sharing on an easy task. Experiment 2 left discussion at the ceiling as well. The open protocol of experiment 3 is our response: it gives agents room to withhold.
  • Reconstructed instructionsWe wrote the wording and quiz items ourselves, and the paper does not say what humans were told about other members' information. The authors may still have the original materials.
  • Partial benchmarkWithout Data A we lack the 0 to 3 vote distribution, majority correctness, and pre-interaction rankings for humans.
  • Rounds for timeWe chose five discussion rounds and ten trading rounds per period. These numbers have no direct counterpart in five minutes of talk or 180 seconds of trading.
