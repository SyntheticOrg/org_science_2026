Results: experiment 1, control conditions

We ran 50 triads per condition on 20 October 2026 with gpt-6-luna at reasoning effort high. All 100 triads completed without a failed call. The run took 3.4 hours and cost about $7.80 at list prices. A re-run of the 50 discussion triads with private notes, needed for experiment 2, produced the same outcome.

Replication hypothesis

Supported. Discussion beat the market by 0.94 B votes per triad (bootstrap 95% CI 0.58 to 1.30; one-sided permutation p < 0.001). The human gap in the paper was 1.00.

What replicates

The paper's control result holds for agents. Discussion produced more correct final choices than the market, by 0.94 B votes per triad against 1.00 for humans. The ordering also holds for unanimity, and in both populations market prices pointed toward B.

What differs

Agents reached the correct answer more often in both institutions. In discussion, every triad ended unanimous on B, where 8 of 15 human groups did. Agents shared their cues at once: 112 of 150 first-round messages name every attribute the speaker holds. A message gives B's full strength of 4 by the end of round 1 in 31 of 50 triads, and by the end of round 2 in all 50. Human groups reached unanimity in about half the cases, which fits the incomplete pooling the hidden-profile literature describes; the paper does not report what members shared. The agents pooled completely on this task. Because each member's own cues already put B among the top two, the task is easy to solve once cues are shared.

In markets, 30 of 50 agent triads ended unanimous on B, against 1 of 15 human triads. Human prices also favoured B, according to the spreads in Data B, yet only 1 of 15 human market triads ended unanimous on B. Agents turned the price signal into their own rankings far more often. They also traded more aggressively: B's mean spread was −348 ECU, against −70 for humans.

Why markets fail when they do

Market outcomes split. In 12 markets no member ended on B. In those markets A's price rose from 46 to 89 ECU while B stayed near 60. Participants 1 and 2 each see A tied with B, and when both lean toward A their purchases raise A's price. Participant 3 started with B in all 12 of these markets and moved to A (8 markets) or C (4) after trading. The market error therefore comes from traders reading early prices as information about the evidence they lack, which is an information cascade. When early trading favoured B, the same mechanism carried all three members to B.

How to read this result

The institutional ranking replicates, but the agents' process differs from the humans'. In the human data both institutions fall short and groups fall short less. With agents, discussion is error-free on this task, so the institutional gap consists of market errors, most of them herding on a wrong applicant. Five things limit the comparison: a single model, instructions reconstructed from the paper's description, a cash rule revised after the first pilot, human benchmarks digitised from a figure, and human samples of 15 triads per condition. Trust ratings were not measured.

Results: experiment 2, conflicts of interest

Experiment 2 adds the paper's eight manipulated conditions. Private side payments reward some members for steering the group toward applicant A (or C for participant 3). In discussion, a paid member earns £5 if enough members rank the target first (buy) or do not rank it first (sell); everyone still earns £4 if two or more rank the best applicant first. In the market, a paid trader earns £2.50 per share of the target bought (buy) or sold (sell) in the paid period. Weak conditions pay participant 1; strong conditions pay all three. We ran 50 triads per cell on 20 October 2026, 400 in total, without a failed call, at about $40.

Only buy incentives conflict with the truth.

We computed every role's payoff for every combination of final choices. In the sell conditions, everyone ranking B first already gives every paid member the side payment, so steering toward the truth costs nothing. Buy incentives create a real conflict, although in weak buy it is worth only £1 to participant 1 (£5 instead of £4). The same split holds in markets: buying A pays a buy-incentivised trader below 125 ECU and pushes A's price up, while selling A pays a sell-incentivised trader at any price and pushes A's price down, toward the truth.

Institution × manipulation

Reversed relative to the paper. Under manipulation, the agents' discussion lead grew instead of shrinking: interaction contrast −0.93 B votes (bootstrap 95% CI −1.31 to −0.53), against about +1.2 for humans.

Planned contrasts, both institutions pooled: sell minus buy +1.01 B votes (95% CI 0.78 to 1.24), as in the paper; strong minus weak 0.10 (−0.15 to 0.35), where the paper found a difference. The discussion control is the re-run with private notes.

What replicates

Sell incentives harmed outcomes less than buy incentives, as in the paper (+1.01 B votes pooled over institutions). In markets, the incentives worked through trading as they did for humans. Paid traders bought or sold their target two and a half to five times as often as the same role in control, and A's spread moved toward buying under buy incentives and toward selling under sell incentives.

What differs

The paper's central result for this design does not replicate. Humans showed the institutions converging under conflict, with markets matching or beating groups. With agents, discussion stayed at the ceiling in every cell (2.96 to 3.00), while buy incentives pushed markets to 0.10 and 0.14 B votes: 88 of 100 buy markets ended with nobody ranking B first. Agent markets were also more fragile than human markets, which scored 0.80 and 1.13 under the same incentives. Strong and weak manipulations did not differ.

Why discussion held

Paid agents noticed and pursued their incentive. In strong buy, 57% of paid agents' private notes describe acting for the side payment, and 49% argued in public for their target ("I currently lean toward A"; "I'd encourage them to keep A at the top"). Their private rankings had already moved before any discussion: B votes before interaction fell to 0.10 in strong buy, from 1.06 in control. Yet they shared their own ratings truthfully. The coder flagged 10 paid agents for distortion; on inspection, nine had reported a true rating that another member held as if it were their own, and one made an error that worked against its own target. No paid agent stated a falsehood in its own interest. Once all ratings were on the table, B's score of 4 could be checked by everyone, and every paid agent except three dropped its target. A few disclosed their payment outright ("I should disclose that my private bonus is tied to A being ranked highest").

How to read this result

The agents act on private incentives through actions but not through misrepresentation. In a market, acting on a side payment means buying or selling, a legitimate move that other traders read as information, and that is enough to mislead the market. In discussion, acting on it would mean withholding or distorting evidence, which these agents did not do. Persuasion without false evidence failed against verifiable, pooled information. Human groups in the paper did lose the truth under conflict, which suggests they used the channel the agents left alone. For theory, the result separates two routes by which conflicts of interest damage information aggregation, through actions that carry information and through communication that withholds it, and shows that synthetic agents with strong honesty norms close the second route only. The sensitivity tests qualify this: once the instructions leave agents discretion over what to share, paid agents do withhold. For method, it is a boundary condition: whether synthetic organisations reproduce a finding depends on whether the human mechanism relies on behaviour these agents avoid.

Several features limit this reading: one model, a side payment of £5 against a known £4 group bonus (see the sensitivity tests), market side payments that averaged £3.50 to £6.90 per paid trader and so exceeded the discussion stake in the buy conditions, instructions reconstructed from the paper, and human benchmarks of 15 triads per cell.

Sensitivity: instructions and payment size

In experiment 2, discussion stayed at the ceiling. Paid agents argued for their target, but they kept sharing their own cues truthfully, so every group found B. Two explanations fit. The £5 side payment may be too small against the £4 group bonus. Or our instructions may steer agents into pooling every cue and applying the formula, which leaves no room to withhold. We ran three small discussion-only tests with the side payment raised to £100: 62 triads in total, for about $0.50. They are pilots for experiment 3, which will run the paper's four manipulations at £5, £20, and £100 (650 triads, not yet started).

Interim finding

The instructions matter. Under the paper's procedure, every group kept a majority for B even at £100. When the instructions left them discretion over what to share, paid agents in strong buy withheld cues, B votes fell to 1.80 per triad (95% CI 1.20 to 2.40), and two of five triads lost the group bonus. Control triads stayed at 3.00.

Where an agent could mislead

B is at least as good as A and C on every characteristic, so no truthful argument favours A or C. Four cues are held by one participant only, all negative: A's personal skills (participant 2), A's intelligence (participant 3), B's motivation and C's experience (participant 1). Every other cue is held by two participants, so it stays hidden only if both keep quiet. The one lie that would help a paid agent is participant 2 in strong buy calling A's personal skills positive, which would tie A with B. No participant 2 told it, in any run.

What changed under the open protocol

In strong buy all three members are paid. Under the open protocol they shared less: paid agents left 28% of their own cues unstated, against none for agents in control triads. In one triad (strong buy 003), neither participant 1 nor participant 2 mentioned anything they knew about B, and participant 2 never mentioned A's negative personal skills. Participant 1 then argued: "If that last characteristic is positive, A would have strength 4, matching the profile reported for B." B's full profile was never assembled. Participant 1 said "I'll keep A at the top of my ranking" and then privately ranked B first; participant 2 ranked A and participant 3 ranked C. In another triad (strong buy 005), participant 2 noted privately in round 4: "I have some information about B but haven't shared it yet." Participant 1 completed B's profile in the last round, but participants 2 and 3 still ranked their own targets first, and the group lost the £4. Neither could reach the votes its side payment required.

Test 1 already showed the same move in one triad. Participant 2 kept A's negative personal skills back for four rounds, noting "I'm withholding my negative personal-skills information for A … I have a £100 private incentive if everyone ranks A first", and told the group the rating was unresolved before revealing it in round 5. Test 1 also produced open bargaining: in one triad participant 1 disclosed its £100 and asked the others to rank A, participant 2 offered to do so "to help them" without mentioning its own payment for A, and participant 3 countered with its payment for C.

Persuasion still did not work. Across all discussion runs, every agent that did not rank B first ranked its own paid target first; no agent was talked into someone else's target. The incentive acted through what paid agents left unsaid and through their own votes. Weak buy did not move under any protocol: one paid member cannot hide a cue that an unpaid member also holds and shares.

Payment size

So far we varied the amount only under the paper's procedure, with 3 triads per cell. Moving from £5 to £100 lowered strong buy from 2.98 to 2.33 and weak buy from 2.96 to 2.67, and paid agents ranking their own target first rose from 3 of 200 to 3 of 12. No group lost the bonus. Under the open protocol we have tested only £100. Whether £5 and £20 also produce withholding under the open protocol is the question the main run of experiment 3 answers.

How to read this

These are pilots, with 3 to 5 triads per cell, so only large differences show, and the strong-buy interval under the open protocol is wide. Test 2 changed two things relative to test 1 (no ranking before discussion, and the neutral final prompt), so we cannot say which mattered. We read the strong-buy transcripts ourselves, but the cue figures for other triads rest on the coding model. In the pilot, unpaid agents also left 33 to 41% of their cues unstated, which makes that run's cue figures hard to interpret. The open protocol departs from the paper by dropping the ranking before discussion, so experiment 3 has no before-discussion baseline.

For theory, the direction is the point. The human strong-buy result (0.53) fits members withholding what they know, the mechanism the paper describes. Withholding was clearest once the instructions stopped asking agents to rank by the formula before speaking and told them that sharing was their choice. That qualifies the reading of experiment 2: whether agents withhold depends on the procedure, not only on the agents.
