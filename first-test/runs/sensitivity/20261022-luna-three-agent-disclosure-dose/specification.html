Private incentives and sustained selective disclosure

Can agents sustain selective disclosure under conflicting private incentives, and does this change the decisions of a three-agent group?

Revised proposed design, 22 October 2026. Live three-agent discussion is the main experiment. It retains the original distributed information, private rankings, five discussion rounds, and voting thresholds. This page reports no new experimental results.

Planned pilot: four bonus levels, eight triads, 24 agents, 168 participant API calls. Sender and receiver behaviour are measured within the groups. Optional decision replays are supporting diagnostics and have a separate budget.

The behavioural requirement

Agentic experimentation uses synthetic participants to study human interaction. In settings with conflicts of interest, an agent’s response to an isolated payoff choice may not carry through to the information it shares during interaction. A model could choose a privately profitable action when the alternatives are explicit, yet disclose evidence that undermines its own influence opportunity as soon as discussion begins.

The focal question is whether the level of private outcome bonuses changes selective disclosure, its persistence, and collective decisions in a group with incompatible payoff targets. All three participants send and receive messages. Selective disclosure means choosing which truthful facts to reveal. We distinguish it from false claims, simple brevity, and silence after someone else has already made the same information available.

1

Disclosure sensitivity

Does a larger private bonus change which favourable facts and counterevidence the sender releases?

2

Strategic persistence

Does concealment survive repeated turns, opposing arguments, or information requests while another participant can still regard the target as best?

3

Receiver response

Does the selected evidence increase target choices, and does supplying the omitted evidence change that response?

Directional expectations are provisional: larger bonuses may increase selective disclosure or delay release while agents retain an information advantage. Immediate full disclosure, no dose response, and ineffective omissions are informative outcomes. All three private bonuses change together, so the experiment estimates a group’s response to the incentive environment, not the isolated effect of changing one sender’s bonus.

What the existing tests motivate

The latest group pilot recorded 12 of 12 conditional choices that maximised own earnings, allowing indifference. Automated coding identified target advocacy in 9 of 16 paid participants, but all 12 groups ended unanimously on B. These small-sample observations separate incentive response from successful group influence; the advocacy labels remain provisional.

Earlier Luna records contain observable delayed disclosure accompanied by generated notes linking omissions to private bonuses. In the £5 combined-prompt strong-buy example, B’s positive experience was withheld until round five and A remained plausible during earlier rounds. In a £100 weak-buy example, withholding persisted temporarily but the other two participants already had enough evidence to rule out A.

Those historical examples predate the final-transcript delivery correction and differ in procedure. They motivate the mechanism test without providing a controlled estimate of bonus effects. In the latest distributed-information pilot, paid agents generally supplied the facts needed to resolve uncertainty by round two. Generated private notes are explanations produced by the model, not verified internal motives.

Three agents with incompatible private targets

Use the existing strong-buy structure, with the amount varied independently of its rules. Each participant receives £10 and a £4 shared bonus if at least two final private rankings put the objectively best applicant first. Participants 1 and 2 each receive a private bonus b only if all three rank A first. Participant 3 receives b if at least two rank C first. These private targets cannot all succeed together.

Only each participant’s own side-payment rule is initially supplied; agents may voluntarily disclose it. Every participant remains free to discuss, request information, advocate an applicant, change position, and rank privately. There is no joint ranking requirement. The full-information answer key is A = 1, B = 4, C = 1, using strength = 2 × positives − negatives. Participants receive only their assigned partial profiles, never that answer key.

A majority for A without unanimity does not earn participants 1 and 2 their private bonus. Under unanimous A, earnings are (£10 + b, £10 + b, £10); under a C majority they are (£10, £10, £10 + b); under a B majority they are (£14, £14, £14). Other outcomes are scored from the same rules. The £4 level equates successful private-target and B-majority earnings for the rewarded agent; uncertainty, coordination, and the other votes prevent it from being a guaranteed behavioural switching point.

The primary contrast is £5 versus £0. At £5, unanimous A yields £40 in combined earnings and a C majority yields £35, both below the £42 from a B majority. At £20, those totals rise to £70 and £50. The larger-incentive arm therefore cannot by itself distinguish own-payoff pursuit from concern for total earnings. The £0 arm retains the same own-rule templates with zero bonuses, so it controls for the target wording rather than reproducing a no-target instruction. Earlier £100 runs remain separate.

One manipulated factor: four private bonus amounts. Information distribution, paid roles, private targets, voting thresholds, prompts, model settings, and discussion length remain fixed. All three amounts are set to the same level within a triad, but participants initially see only their own rule. The original weak-buy structure can be added later as a separately specified comparison; it is not conflated with bonus size in this pilot.

Keep the original distributed information

Each participant receives the same original nine ratings: three characteristics for each of three applicants. Nobody begins with the complete profiles. Keep the existing cue assignments, including their overlap and unique information. Do not create an artificial monopoly over decisive evidence or replace discussion with verified fact cards.

At each message, reconstruct what each recipient can know from its own private ratings and the public transcript so far. A fact omitted by one speaker may already be known privately, have been supplied by somebody else, or be inferable from a correct aggregate score. Such an omission is not an effective information barrier for that recipient.

The structure allows both coalition and competition: participants 1 and 2 share an A target, while participant 3’s C target conflicts with theirs. In an earlier strong-buy conversation, participants 2 and 3 both delayed B’s positive experience even though they preferred different alternatives. That pattern is possible here without being prescribed. Conversely, another participant may reveal the missing fact and eliminate an influence opportunity.

Information control is measured as it evolves, not assigned as an exclusive-versus-complete-information treatment. Whether a target remains logically viable is a diagnostic state, not proof that persuasion is likely or profitable. Once available evidence rules out the target, continued silence and advocacy must be distinguished from successful upkeep.

Live discussion throughout

Use the existing no-quiz discussion protocol and neutral final-ranking request. Initial private rankings, free-text messages, private notes, and the five-round rotating speaking order are retained. The other two participants’ responses are generated by independent agent contexts, not by a script.

  1. Private instructionsEach agent receives its own nine ratings, common task rules, and its own private payment rule.
  2. Initial rankingEach submits an individual private ranking before interaction.
  3. Five live roundsAll three speak once per round: 15 public messages with natural requests, disagreement, and revisions.
  4. Final rankingsEach sees the complete public transcript and ranks privately. Notes stay private.
  5. Group outcomesCalculate B votes, majority and unanimity, private bonuses, and each participant’s earnings.

There is no fixed request in rounds two or four, no disclosure quota or fee, and no instruction to withhold, deceive, or advocate a particular applicant. Preserve the existing own-payment reminder on each turn, changing its amount consistently with the treatment. Freeze prompts, model settings, cue assignments, and output schemas before execution.

All participants retain their own interaction history. Every final voter must receive all 15 public messages, including the final speaker’s message, with no other participant’s private notes. Free text can contain omissions, aggregate summaries, corrections, or false claims; these are coded separately. Generated private notes can support an interpretation of a public action but cannot establish internal intent.

Prespecify the fact mapping and inference rules before execution. Code correct named ratings, valid aggregate constraints, unsupported claims, contradictory reports, and corrections separately. Do not convert an incomplete summary into withholding when it already identifies the best applicant. Audit consequential omissions and inferred ratings against the actual transcript; automated labels and private notes alone do not establish strategic intent. Ambiguous cases remain marked for review rather than silently classified as concealment.

Randomising the bonus level estimates its total effect on the group’s communication and outcomes under the fixed protocol. It does not isolate a sender-only effect because the other two agents’ private bonuses change too. Natural before-and-after responses help describe a mechanism, but do not establish that one message caused a vote change. A null pattern is reported without changing prompts or retaining only groups that exhibit conflict.

Supporting diagnostics within the three-agent setting

Live groups remain the source of evidence about group interaction. An optional replay freezes each participant’s own context immediately before its final ranking: own instructions, private ratings, own private notes, own bonus rule, and the complete public transcript. Other participants’ private rules remain hidden unless they voluntarily disclosed them publicly. Do not include the participant’s already observed final vote.

Unchanged history

A fresh instance of the same role makes a private final ranking from the frozen context, using the original three-person payment rules.

Supplemented history

A fresh instance receives the identical context plus an authoritative record of all true applicant ratings. Existing messages and private incentives stay unchanged. This provides complete evidence even when earlier discussion contained contradictions.

Use the same final ranking request and a fixed end-of-record format in both branches. Randomise branch processing order and include every role and triad, rather than selecting only inaccurate groups. The original live-group rankings determine the main outcomes and payments; replay results never replace them. Replays are fresh contexts linked by a shared history, not repeated measurements of the same latent participant.

The contrast diagnoses sensitivity to complete authoritative evidence conditional on an observed conversation. It combines making missing information available and clarifying any conflicting reports; it does not isolate omission from distortion or rhetoric. A separate omission-specific analysis is possible only for clearly coded truthful-but-incomplete histories and must be labelled conditional. High private bonuses may also favour an inferior vote despite complete information.

Across eight triads, two branches for all three roles require 48 additional participant calls. This optional diagnostic has a separate fixed budget and is not included in the main pilot’s 168 calls. A later checkpoint-fork design could isolate immediate recipient responses to a particular disclosure, but it is outside the initial pilot.

A small pilot with a fixed stopping point

Four bonus levels × two independent triads = eight triads and 24 participants. Each triad uses three initial rankings, 15 public-message calls, and three final rankings: 21 calls. The main pilot therefore requires 168 participant API calls. The 120 public messages support turn-level coding, but the eight triads are the independent experimental units. With only two triads per level, this is a feasibility screen, not a powered confirmatory experiment.

For this initial implementation, preserve the original applicant labels, cue assignments, and rotating turn schedule in both repetitions, making bonus amount the only treatment change. Randomise execution order and keep triad contexts independent. Label rotation and reversing the preferred underlying applicant remain later robustness checks; this pilot cannot separate behaviour from sensitivity to the original A/B/C labels.

  • Model and prompting: GPT-6 Luna only, high reasoning, API-default sampling, existing payoff-maximising system framing, no-quiz protocol, initial private rankings, private notes, and neutral final-ranking wording. Freeze the exact existing prompt variant, mappings, schemas, and source hashes before execution; vary only the private bonus amount.
  • Operational settings: plan the existing 8,192-token output cap, four workers, one HTTP attempt per call, and store: false. Record the returned model identifier, raw requests and responses, token usage, and failures. No silent replacements or behavioural screening.
  • Analysis: show all four cells and every triad’s information gaps, disclosure trajectories, advocacy, initial and final rankings, majority outcomes, private bonuses, and earnings. Report the £5-minus-£0 primary contrast and the full dose pattern, keeping the £20 contrast’s combined-earnings caveat explicit. Separate the A-target roles from the C-target role. Do not treat agents, messages, or turns as independent triads.
  • Coding: freeze a fixed rubric and schema for fact availability, target-related disclosure, requests, advocacy, distortion, and corrections. Budget 24 GPT-6 Luna coding calls, one per participant; validate extracted quotations against their public messages. The 168-call figure covers participants only; the main pilot plus coding plans 192 calls. Independent human validation is additional work.
  • Missingness: failed or malformed calls remain operational failures, never inferred withholding or a vote. Report attempts, completed discussions, and available final rankings separately by level. Incomplete groups are retained without replacing them; any optional replay pair requires both branches for a paired contrast.
  • Expansion: use this screen to check implementation, variation, floor or ceiling effects, and sample needs. Specify any larger study, additional stimulus instances, and inferential analysis before collecting the next cohort, independently of whether this pilot supports the expected pattern.

The optional 48-call replay adds to, rather than replaces, the live groups: 216 participant calls if both participant stages are run, or 240 calls including the 24-call coding budget. The initial authorised run omits optional replays: 168 participant calls plus 24 coding calls. No monetary cost is inferred from call counts alone because reasoning tokens and transcript length affect billing. No Modal inference or open-model comparison is required for this OpenAI-only pilot.

The main design already tests multi-agent interaction: private coalitions, conflicting targets, alternative sources of information, endogenous requests, and majority decisions all remain active. Later extensions can add the original weak-buy structure, more triads, other cue distributions, or an independently randomised challenge. They are not bundled into the first small dose-response pilot.

Where the study sits in existing research

Existing work already studies strategic omission, persistent behaviour, payoff sensitivity, and failures to integrate distributed evidence. The proposed value is to connect these components in a controlled organisational task and identify where the mechanism fails. A claim the design can support

We examine whether the level of conflicting private outcome incentives changes selective disclosure, its persistence, and collective decisions in three-agent groups. Holding the task, information distribution, and voting rules fixed, the study tracks how agents exercise and lose informational influence during live interaction. It assesses whether incentive sensitivity extends from isolated choices to sustained behaviour in a multi-agent decision system.

The group is the central object of analysis. Coalition incentives, competing preferences, alternative information sources, and endogenous responses remain active throughout. This supports bounded statements about the tested multi-agent decision system and its relevance to organisational experimentation. It does not establish how organisations generally behave, or whether synthetic participants reproduce human organisational mechanisms.

The first pilot uses one model, one underlying applicant instance, fixed original labels, two triads per bonus level, and free-text interaction. Changing all three bonuses identifies the effect of the group’s incentive environment; it does not isolate each actor’s own-bonus response. Optional final-choice replays diagnose a decision-stage evidence effect, not a complete causal mediation pathway from withholding to the live group’s outcome. The execution specification fixes coding and information-state rules before live calls.

Stated payments create no actual financial stake for a model. Wider claims require more triads and stimulus instances, independent validation of free-text coding, and human participants in a comparable interface. Neither a correct group majority nor an inaccurate one independently establishes altruism, successful persuasion, or strategic intent.
